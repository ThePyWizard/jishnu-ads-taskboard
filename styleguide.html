The Art of Readable Typography

A comprehensive design style guide showcasing every HTML element and design pattern. Built with iA Writer Quattro for body text and Inter for headings, this reference covers typography, layout, media, and interactive components.

Typography

Good typography is invisible. The reader should never have to struggle with the mechanics of reading. Instead, the shapes of letters, the rhythm of spacing, and the clarity of hierarchy should guide the eye effortlessly from one idea to the next.

Body Text

The ideal line length for body text falls between 45 and 75 characters. At this width, the eye can comfortably track from the end of one line to the beginning of the next without losing its place. The typeface, size, and leading all work together to create a reading rhythm that feels natural.

A second paragraph demonstrates the vertical spacing between blocks of text. The gap should be large enough to signal a new thought but small enough to maintain the flow of the argument. One rem of bottom margin achieves this balance at our base size of 22 pixels.

Lead Paragraph

Lead paragraphs introduce a section with slightly larger, lighter text. They give the reader a sense of what follows and set the tone before the body copy takes over.

Small Text & Captions

Small text is useful for disclaimers, legal notes, and secondary information that supports the main content without competing for attention.

Caption text — typically used beneath images, charts, and tables to provide context and attribution.

Inline Formatting

Text can be bold for emphasis, italic for tone or titles, underlined when necessary, or struck through to indicate revision. You can nest them: bold italic carries maximum emphasis.

Use superscript for footnote references1 and subscript for chemical formulae like H2O.

The mark element highlights relevant passages when you want to draw attention to specific text within a larger block.

Abbreviations like CSS and HTML use a dotted underline to indicate that a definition is available on hover.

Keyboard shortcuts are styled with the kbd element: press Cmd + S to save, or Ctrl + Z to undo.

Links

Links are underlined with a thin, offset line. Internal links connect to other sections within this document. External links include a subtle arrow indicator to signal that clicking will navigate away from the current page.

Hover over any link to see the color shift from dark to mid-gray, providing clear interactive feedback without being distracting.

Definition List

Kerning
The adjustment of space between individual letter pairs to achieve visually consistent spacing.
Leading
The vertical distance between lines of text, measured from baseline to baseline.
Tracking
Uniform adjustment of spacing across a range of characters, affecting the overall density of a text block.
X-height
The height of lowercase letters, typically measured using the letter "x." A larger x-height improves readability at small sizes.

Details & Summary

What is a typographic scale?

A typographic scale is a sequence of font sizes derived from a consistent ratio. Much like a musical scale creates harmony through mathematical relationships between notes, a type scale creates visual harmony through proportional sizing. Common ratios include 1.2 (minor third), 1.25 (major third), and 1.333 (perfect fourth).

Why use two typeface families?

Pairing a serif or monospaced body face with a sans-serif heading face creates clear visual distinction between content levels. The contrast helps readers instantly identify headings, making the document easier to scan. iA Writer Quattro and Inter complement each other because they share a similar x-height and open letterforms.

How does line-height affect readability?

Line-height (leading) controls the vertical space between lines. Too tight, and lines blur together. Too loose, and the text feels disconnected. For body text at 16–24px, a line-height of 1.5 to 1.7 generally works well. The ideal value depends on line length, font size, and the typeface's built-in spacing.

Footnotes

Footnotes provide supplementary information without interrupting the main text. References appear as superscript numbers2 that link to notes at the bottom of the document. They are particularly useful in academic and editorial contexts3 where attribution matters.

Meta Band

An asymmetric header band: a content-width lead block beside a flexible fill partner that takes the remaining width, split by a hairline rule. Use it for a hero figure, counter, or KPI paired with supporting detail. Stacks vertically on mobile.

The fill partner absorbs all remaining width, so its heading sets on one line instead of wrapping mid-phrase.

Principle. Size the lead to its content, not a proportion like flex: 2 — grow inflates the box and opens whitespace beside the content. Let one partner fill (flex: 1 1 auto; min-width: 0), and cap captions with max-width so they wrap rather than dictating the column width. Verify layout visually (a screenshot or bounding-box check), not just in the DOM.

Overlays & Dialogs

Overlay components layer above content to communicate, confirm, or extend the interface — without requiring navigation. Everything here is hand-rolled, dependency-free, and fully keyboard-accessible. The governing question is always: does this overlay serve the reader, or does it ambush them? Components that serve are documented below. Components that ambush are in the anti-patterns appendix at the bottom of this section.

Press ⌘K / Ctrl+K anywhere on this page to open the command palette.

Core Dialogs

Confirm Dialog

Interrupts for a binary decision. Reserve for destructive or irreversible actions — not for low-stakes confirmation. Uses role="alertdialog" so screen readers announce it immediately on open.

Delete this document? alertdialog

This action cannot be undone. The document and all its history will be permanently removed.

Alert Dialog

Non-dismissable notice requiring acknowledgment. Blocks all interaction until the user responds. Use sparingly — only when the user absolutely must act before continuing.

Session expired alertdialog

Your session has timed out. Log in again to continue.

Prompt Dialog

A dialog with a single text input. Use for quick inline naming or values — not as a substitute for a proper form page.

Rename document

Form Dialog

Multi-field dialog for short forms that don't warrant a full page. Keep it under five fields — anything more belongs in a settings page.

Add team member

Non-modal Dialog

A floating informational panel that does not trap focus or block the page. role="dialog" without aria-modal. Use for help panels, inspectors, or feature-preview sidecars the user may want to reference while continuing work.

Transient

Toast

Brief centered feedback that auto-dismisses after a few seconds. Errors and toasts with an action do not auto-dismiss — they wait for explicit user response. Uses a persistent aria-live="polite" region so screen readers announce without relocating focus.

Tooltip

Anti-Patterns

The following patterns are documented here precisely so you can identify and avoid them. They degrade trust, damage the reading experience, and are hostile to the users they claim to serve.

Anti-pattern: Timed Interstitial

A full-screen blocking overlay appearing on page load — before the user has read anything or expressed any intent. Usually deployed to collect email addresses or promote a product. The user must dismiss it to access the content they came for. This pattern demonstrably increases bounce rate, erodes trust, and suppresses return visits. That it "converts" is an artifact of dark-pattern friction, not genuine interest.

Never build this. Surface a signup prompt contextually in the content, after the user has read enough to have formed intent.

Anti-pattern: Exit-Intent Popup

A dialog triggered when the cursor approaches the top of the browser window, interpreted as an intent to leave. It is a manipulation pattern that weaponizes a false signal — cursor movement is not exit intent. It interrupts users who are scrolling up, switching tabs, or reaching for the address bar. The popup appears at exactly the moment the user has decided the page offers them nothing. A popup cannot recover that decision.

If your content isn't compelling enough to retain readers, fix the content.

Anti-pattern: Cookie / GDPR Guilt-Wall

A consent UI designed to make "Accept All" the path of least resistance — prominent button, large target — while burying "Reject" behind multiple clicks, grey text, or a separate settings page. This is manufactured consent, and is illegal under GDPR's equal-ease requirement. A compliant consent UI makes Accept and Reject equally accessible in a single step. If your product cannot survive users choosing privacy, the product has a business model problem, not a UI problem.

Morning Routine for Better Focus

A short guide to structuring your first hour for deep, sustained concentration throughout the day.

Most productivity advice focuses on tools and systems. But the foundation of focused work is physiological — how you wake, move, and fuel your brain in the first sixty minutes sets the tone for everything that follows.

Key Principle

Delay caffeine by 90 minutes after waking. Adenosine levels need time to clear naturally; early caffeine merely postpones the crash.

The Three Pillars

  1. Light exposure — Get 10 minutes of natural sunlight within 30 minutes of waking to anchor your circadian rhythm.
  2. Movement — A brief walk or stretch sequence raises core temperature and primes the prefrontal cortex.
  3. Single-tasking — Protect your first work block from meetings, messages, and notifications.
Common Mistake

Checking email or social media before your first focused block floods your working memory with other people's priorities. Treat the first hour as yours.

The world is full of obvious things which nobody by any chance ever observes.

Sample weekly schedule

Monday through Friday, allocate 6:30–7:30 AM as a protected block. Weekends can shift later but should maintain the light-move-focus sequence. Consistency matters more than the exact hour.

For further reading, see Andrew Huberman's protocols on morning routines, or explore the accordion section above for more on typographic structure.
